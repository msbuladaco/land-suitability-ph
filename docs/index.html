The project

Why land suitability matters

Land evaluation is “the process of assessment of land performance when used for specific purposes” (FAO, 1976). Land suitability analysis assesses the inherent capacity of a land unit, here a soil type, to support a specific use. Matching land characteristics to crop requirements helps farmers choose crops that fit their soil, climate and terrain, which raises productivity, lowers risk and supports food security.

The project builds suitability models for 17 major crops at the provincial level. It combines a validated compendium of crop requirements with satellite-derived land cover, interpolated climate surfaces, soil and topographic layers, and expert-derived criteria weights (AHP / Fuzzy-AHP), then overlays them in ArcGIS Pro.

Objectives

  1. Establish a validated compendium of crop requirements for major crops in the Philippines.
  2. Generate land cover and NDVI maps at the provincial level using appropriate image classification techniques.
  3. Generate climatic criteria maps using the best-fit interpolation method.
  4. Generate soil and topographic criteria maps using secondary data and GIS tools.
  5. Derive criteria and sub-criteria weights using AHP or Fuzzy-AHP.
  6. Generate actual and potential suitability maps for the major crops at the provincial level.
  7. Recommend ways to address the limitations to productivity and suitability.

Study 1 · Results

Compendium of Land Utilization Type Requirements

Starting from Sys et al. (1993), the team searched scholarly databases, journals and government publications for crop requirements, assessed each source’s credibility, and delineated the value ranges for every land characteristic into FAO suitability classes. The result is a compendium of class thresholds for 17 major crops covering climate, soil physical and chemical properties, and terrain.

Nested class envelopes from the source workbook were converted into explicit, non-overlapping intervals that can be used directly to reclassify GIS layers. Where a source gives only one “N” class, the split between N1 and N2 is not inferred and is marked ND.

Study leader: Jessabel B. Magtoltol · Co-study leader: Hiyasmin Rose L. Benzon

Methodology

How the compendium was built

1

Base framework

Crop requirement tables of Sys et al. (1993) and the FAO Framework for Land Evaluation (FAO, 1976; 2007) set the structure of factors and classes.

2

Literature search

Scholarly databases, journals and agency publications in agriculture, agronomy, plant science and crop production were searched for each crop.

3

Source appraisal

Each source was assessed for credibility and quality. Philippine field studies and BSWM suitability products were prioritized where available.

4

Class delineation

Value ranges per parameter were delineated into S1–N2 and converted from nested envelopes into explicit, GIS-ready intervals.

5

Independent validation

FAO GAEZ, ECOCROP, FAO lowland-rice examples, BSWM maps and recent studies served as a quality-control layer. Disagreements are flagged, not averaged.

Application cautions

  • These are screening thresholds, not yield guarantees. Cultivar, planting season, irrigation, fertilization, pests and data resolution all affect real suitability.
  • Annual climate means can hide seasonal extremes. Cabbage, carrot, onion and potato should be read against the actual growing season or highland environment.
  • Coffee combines Arabica and Robusta. Mapping should separate the two where elevation and temperature data allow.
  • Lowland rice reverses the usual drainage preference: controlled ponding on fine-textured, slowly permeable soils is favorable for paddy.
  • N1 vs N2 depends partly on management and economics. Acidity, salinity or slope may improve; bedrock depth or extreme terrain usually cannot.
